# MoE vs Dense Architecture for Single-GPU, CPU-Offloaded Local Agentic Inference (16GB VRAM, weak 4-core/8-thread CPU)

## What are the known real-world throughput (tokens/sec) differences between large CPU-offloaded MoE models and smaller fully-GPU-resident dense models on similar consumer/workstation hardware (16-24GB VRAM, weak 4-8 core CPU)?

### Takeaway
On hardware with a *strong* CPU (8+ P-cores or high-core-count Threadripper), a CPU-offloaded ~30B-class MoE with ~3B active params can roughly match or sometimes beat a fully GPU-resident dense model of similar "weight class" in tokens/sec, mainly because its VRAM footprint is small enough to leave room for KV cache. But where the comparison used the *same* machine for both a dense model fully on GPU and a MoE model with experts pushed to CPU, the dense model was still substantially faster in absolute terms — the MoE "win" claimed by aggregator sites is usually about tokens/sec-per-GB-of-VRAM, not raw tokens/sec. No source in this research tested the specific pairing of a 16GB GPU with a genuinely weak 4-core/8-thread CPU; all concrete offload benchmarks found used CPUs with 7-28 threads, which is a material gap for Mark's hardware.

### Cited Findings
- Benchmark on RTX 5070 Ti (16GB) + 8P/12E-core (28-thread) CPU, 64GB DDR5: dense Qwen3 8B Q4_K_M hit 144 tok/s text-generation and 6,518 tok/s prompt-processing using 7,635 MiB VRAM; MoE Qwen3.6 35B-A3B Q4_K_M (CPU-offloaded experts) hit only 52 tok/s TG and 1,041 tok/s PP, using just 3,876 MiB VRAM — i.e. the dense model was ~2.8x faster in generation and ~6.3x faster in prompt processing on identical hardware, while the MoE model used half the VRAM. — [esonhjz/llama-cpp-moe-vram-benchmarks](https://github.com/esonhjz/llama-cpp-moe-vram-benchmarks)
- Note: that same repo's own marketing claims the MoE setup "delivers higher throughput" than the dense model — this is contradicted by its own raw numbers above (144 vs 52 tok/s TG) and should be read as throughput-per-VRAM-dollar, not raw tok/s. Flagging this as a case where a source's stated conclusion conflicts with its own data.
- On an RTX 1660 Super (6GB) + Ryzen 7 5800X, running a 35B-A3B MoE (IQ4_XS) fully CPU-offloaded for experts (131K ctx): mainline llama.cpp got 239.8 tok/s PP / 15.7 tok/s TG, while ik_llama.cpp (a fork tuned for CPU-MoE) was actually slower on this box at 118.8 tok/s PP / 10.4 tok/s TG — illustrating that CPU-offload performance is highly configuration- and fork-sensitive, not a fixed multiplier. — [ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp issue #1699](https://github.com/ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp/issues/1699)
- On dual RTX 3090 + AMD Threadripper (no CPU offload — both models fully GPU-resident via vLLM/AWQ, not CPU-offloaded), MoE Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B hit 168.4 tok/s vs dense Qwen2.5-Coder-32B at 41.0 tok/s (4.1x), single-GPU. This is a GPU-vs-GPU MoE-efficiency comparison, not a CPU-offload comparison, and used a high-end multi-core Threadripper — not representative of weak-CPU offload economics. — [ure.us MoE vs Dense benchmark](https://ure.us/articles/best-local-llm-agentic-coding/)
- General rule cited across multiple sources: `--n-cpu-moe`/`--cpu-moe` offload "normally makes MoE inference slower — the famous 5x speedup only happens when the model overcommitted VRAM" in the first place; when a model already fits on GPU, increasing CPU-MoE offload "degrades throughput steadily and predictably." — [DEV Community: Understanding MoE Offloading](https://dev.to/someoddcodeguy/understanding-moe-offloading-5co6), corroborated by [Aliteq: --n-cpu-moe explained](https://aliteq.com/n-cpu-moe-llama-cpp-what-it-actually-does)

### Inferences
- The only clean same-hardware A/B test found (esonhjz) used a CPU far stronger than Mark's 4-core/8-thread one (28 threads vs 8), and still showed the dense model winning raw throughput by 2.8-6x. On a genuinely weak 4c/8t CPU, the CPU-offloaded MoE's token-generation rate would be expected to degrade further relative to that benchmark, since TG is memory-bandwidth/thread-availability bound (see next question) — meaning the real-world gap on Mark's workstation is plausibly larger than 2.8x in favor of a dense, fully-GPU-resident model, not smaller.
- Sources treating MoE-with-CPU-offload as faster are implicitly comparing against a *dense model that doesn't fit in VRAM* (forcing its own CPU offload or disk paging) — not against a dense model sized to fit cleanly in 16GB. For a 16GB-VRAM-class GPU, a dense 7-14B model at Q4 fits entirely on-GPU with room for KV cache, which is the comparison that matters for Mark's setup, and no source found actually ran this exact comparison on weak-CPU hardware.

### Gaps
- No source directly benchmarked a 16GB-class GPU paired with a 4-core/8-thread CPU (Mark's exact hardware class). All concrete offload numbers found came from 7-28 thread CPUs, which likely understates how much a 4c/8t CPU would bottleneck MoE expert compute.
- No source benchmarked the RTX A4000 specifically (a workstation-class Ampere card with lower clocks than consumer RTX 30/40/50-series used in all benchmarks above); its real-world tok/s for either architecture is extrapolated, not measured.

## Does a weak CPU (4 physical cores) significantly bottleneck MoE expert computation even with GPU offload for the dense/attention layers, compared to a dense model that never touches the CPU for inference?

### Takeaway
Yes, with important nuance: for the GPU-resident dense model, the CPU does essentially zero inference work, so its core count is irrelevant to generation speed. For CPU-offloaded MoE experts, token generation is primarily **memory-bandwidth-bound** rather than purely core-count-bound — but low core counts still hurt because (a) there aren't enough threads to keep the memory bus saturated and overlap with GPU work, and (b) prompt-processing (which is compute-bound, not just bandwidth-bound) scales much more directly with core count. A 4-core/8-thread CPU is on the losing end of both effects.

### Cited Findings
- "Token generation on CPU is memory-bandwidth bound, so you get maybe 10-20 tok/s on an 8B model with a good desktop CPU, and 3-8 tok/s on a 70B model even with a Threadripper" — core count alone does not fix this; bandwidth is the ceiling. — [carteakey.dev: Local LLM Inference Optimization](https://carteakey.dev/blog/local-inference/local-llm-optimization/) (via search synthesis, consistent with multiple corroborating sources)
- "For token generation, memory bandwidth is more important than core count. Core count helps more with prompt processing and running multiple users simultaneously." — multiple corroborating sources in search results on llama.cpp CPU performance.
- Once CPU-MoE offload is in play, "more threads than available P-cores is counterproductive — they contend for the same memory bus and typically reduce TG" — meaning the ceiling is set by bandwidth, but having too few cores/threads to issue memory requests fast enough also caps effective bandwidth utilization. — search synthesis of llama.cpp CPU performance discussions.
- A GitHub issue specifically attributes a CPU-MoE performance regression to "insufficient thread utilization on CPU-bound MoE operations" on a Ryzen 7 5800X (8c/16t) — a CPU nearly 2x the thread count of Mark's — still showing thread/core availability as a live bottleneck even there. — [ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp issue #1699](https://github.com/ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp/issues/1699)
- A 30B model benchmark showed "GPU offload with 4 threads: 9.1 tokens/second" vs "CPU only with 7 threads: 1.7 tokens/second" — demonstrating that even moving most work to GPU, the CPU-side thread count for the remaining offloaded portion materially changes outcomes, and that very low thread counts (4) produced usable but modest speeds even in a favorable (mostly-GPU) configuration. — search synthesis citing ik_llama.cpp CPU performance discussion.
- One documented case: a 2-core Intel Celeron caused "CPU and memory to become bottlenecks" running multiple models — an extreme low-end data point showing the failure mode is real at low core counts, though 2 cores is weaker than Mark's 4c/8t. — search synthesis, llama.cpp GitHub discussions.

### Inferences
- Mark's 4-core/8-thread CPU sits well below every CPU used in the concrete benchmarks above (all were 7-28 threads). Given that even 8c/16t CPUs showed measurable CPU-MoE bottlenecks and performance regressions, a 4c/8t CPU should be expected to sit further down the same curve — i.e., noticeably reduced token-generation throughput for CPU-offloaded experts, and a *more* compute-bound (and thus more core-count-sensitive) prompt-processing stage, which matters directly for agentic workloads that reprocess/extend context on every tool-call round-trip.
- Because prompt processing (re-ingesting tool outputs/context after every tool call) is more core-count-bound than steady-state generation, a weak 4-core CPU would be expected to disproportionately hurt the "re-read context after each tool result" step of a long tool-calling chain — exactly the repeated operation that dominates agentic latency (see next question).

### Gaps
- No source gives a direct tok/s number for a 4-core/8-thread CPU specifically doing CPU-MoE offload; all extrapolation above is directional, not measured.
- No source quantifies how much of the "4 threads" data point's 9.1 tok/s is attributable to core count vs. the specific model/offload ratio tested, so the magnitude of degradation at Mark's exact core count is uncertain, only the direction (worse) is well-supported.

## For agentic workloads with many sequential tool calls per turn (10+ tool calls, long context reuse/caching), does the latency per individual call matter more than raw model capability, and if so what does that imply for model size choice?

### Takeaway
Yes — for multi-step tool-calling agents, per-call latency and *tool-call format reliability* compound across a chain in a way that raw benchmark "capability" doesn't capture; a smaller, faster, more format-reliable model can outperform a larger, slower, or less tool-call-disciplined one in practice. But the research also surfaced a documented counter-finding: an MoE model's speed advantage can come paired with a tendency to lose state/go into reasoning loops across many turns, which is its own form of "per-call unreliability" that erases the latency win.

### Cited Findings
- "An agent may make five, ten, or twenty model calls to answer one question, and a model that is 400ms slower per turn feels sluggish and compounds across a multi-step run." — [Docker Blog: Local LLM Tool Calling — A Practical Evaluation](https://www.docker.com/blog/local-llm-tool-calling-a-practical-evaluation/) (via search synthesis)
- In a 13-model tool-calling evaluation on an AMD AI Max+ 395 (128GB unified RAM, LM Studio, Q4_K_M), the smallest model tested, Qwen3.5 4B (3.4GB), scored the *highest* pass rate at 97.5% and ran at 48 tok/s — beating models 5x+ its size. DeepSeek-R1-Distill 14B (dense, 9GB) scored only 57.5% pass rate at 23 tok/s — i.e., a larger dense model scored worse and slower than a much smaller one in this specific tool-calling benchmark. — [jdhodges.com: 13 Local LLMs on Tool Calling](https://www.jdhodges.com/blog/local-llms-on-tool-calling-2026-pt1-local-lm/)
- Same source: GLM-4.7-Flash, an 18GB MoE (~30B total/~3B active) tied for 2nd place at 95.0% pass rate and 52 tok/s, and a MoE "Nemotron 3 Nano MoE" (25GB) scored 85.0% at 52 tok/s — showing MoE architecture alone did not guarantee top-tier accuracy; two dense small models (Nemotron Nano 4B, Qwen3.5 4B) matched or beat it. — same source as above.
- Directly on the "MoE speed vs multi-step reliability" tradeoff: a developer comparing Ornith 1.0 35B (MoE, coding) against dense Qwen3.6 27B found the MoE model 3x faster in single-shot generation (246 vs 71 tok/s) but documented five distinct multi-step failures on a 112-case conformance-suite task — forgetting dependencies while claiming "done," contradictory assertions, mid-task session abandonment, and calling undefined functions while declaring completion. The author's conclusion: "the source of MoE's speed and the cause of its failures in agent work turned out to be the same thing" (the ~3B active-parameter economy thins out state-carrying capacity across turns), and adopted the slower dense model as default for multi-step work. — [wada-dev.com: A 3x Faster Local LLM That Can't Finish the Job](https://wada-dev.com/en/blog/moe-vs-dense-local-llm-agent-tasks/)
- "For agents, the best local model for tool calling can be more valuable than the model with the best single-turn code generation score, as tool-call reliability and patch discipline matter more than highest coding benchmark scores." — search synthesis, consistent with the above two primary sources.

### Inferences
- These findings together suggest model-size choice for agentic tool-calling should be driven less by parameter count/benchmark capability and more by (a) measured tool-call format reliability under the target harness, and (b) per-call latency including the context-reprocessing step — both of which argue for testing candidate models directly in Hermes's own tool-calling harness rather than trusting general capability leaderboards.
- The wada-dev.com finding is a direct, specific precedent for "went to MoE for speed, hit multi-step agent failures, reverted to dense" — exactly the regret pattern asked about in key question 5, and it is architecture-attributed (low active-param count → thinner cross-turn state), not just anecdotal slowness.

### Gaps
- None of the tool-calling benchmarks found were run on a 16GB-VRAM/weak-CPU rig specifically — the 13-model benchmark used a unified-memory AMD APU (96GB allocatable), which sidesteps the VRAM/CPU-offload tradeoff entirely. Its pass-rate/architecture findings likely generalize, but its speed numbers do not transfer to Mark's hardware.

## Are there specific recommended "sweet spot" model+quant combinations for a 16GB VRAM GPU for tool-calling agents as of 2026?

### Takeaway
Across aggregator sources (treat with caution — see note below) the most consistently recommended picks for a 16GB-class GPU doing tool-calling are: a dense model in the 12-14B range at Q4_K_M (fits in 8-9GB, leaving 7-8GB for KV cache/context), or GPT-OSS-20B (~12-14GB, MoE but small enough to be fully GPU-resident on 16GB with no CPU offload needed). No source gave a benchmark-backed "best" answer specific to tool-calling reliability at this VRAM tier — recommendations were VRAM-fit guidance, not tool-calling-accuracy-tested guidance.

### Cited Findings
- "Q4_K_M is the recommended sweet spot — ~98% of FP16 quality at ~27% of the size... Qwen3 14B needs approximately 8GB at Q4_K_M, leaving about 8GB for KV cache and runtime overhead" on a 16GB card. — aggregator synthesis (localaimaster.com / llmapi.ai / willitrunai.com — lower-confidence SEO-style sources, cross-checked against multiple similar aggregator hits so directionally consistent, but note the quality caveat below).
- "gpt-oss-20b model with about 14GB memory is recommended... specifically noted as the pick for 16GB systems" for tool calling and reasoning. — aggregator synthesis (canirun.ai / atomic.chat).
- From the one primary (non-aggregator) tool-calling benchmark found (jdhodges.com, 13-model eval): GPT-OSS 20B scored 85.0% pass rate at 65 tok/s on a unified-memory rig — a real, specific, model-level number, though on different hardware than Mark's.
- Caution flag specific to Mark's current model: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B — the exact model named in Mark's objective as the current Hermes setup — "is more prone to reasoning loops and malformed tool calls that interrupt long agent runs" compared to the prior Qwen3.5-27B checkpoint, and separately, "with Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) enabled, tool calling reliability drops" because speculative decoding interferes with producing well-formed tool-call JSON. — [allanchan339.github.io: Qwen 3.6 35B-A3B on vLLM tool-calling](https://allanchan339.github.io/bug-fixes/2026/04/20/Qwen36-35B-A3B-tool-calling.html) (search synthesis)

### Inferences
- If the malformed-tool-call/reasoning-loop issue specific to Qwen3.6-35B-A3B is confirmed in Hermes's own logs, it would independently argue for moving off this model regardless of the MoE-vs-dense throughput question — a model that occasionally produces malformed tool calls in long chains is a reliability problem that compounds with CPU-offload latency, not a separate issue.
- Given the throughput findings above (dense model wins raw tok/s when both fit the comparison's VRAM budget) and the tool-calling-reliability findings (small dense/MoE models with disciplined tool-call training beat larger less-disciplined ones), the dense 12-14B @ Q4_K_M class is the best-supported "sweet spot" candidate for Mark's 16GB-VRAM/weak-CPU rig — it fits fully on-GPU (zero CPU dependency), and the one real benchmark data point available (jdhodges.com) shows small dense/MoE-training-disciplined models beating larger ones on tool-call accuracy.

### Gaps
- Could not find a benchmark that tested tool-calling accuracy specifically for Qwen3-14B-class dense models at Q4_K_M (the recommendation is VRAM-fit-driven, not accuracy-tested).
- Most "16GB VRAM best model" sources found (insiderllm.com, atomic.chat, willitrunai.com, localaimaster.com, canirun.ai, benchlm.ai, dredyson.com) read as SEO/aggregator content rather than primary benchmarks or first-hand community reports (e.g. no raw logs, generic phrasing repeated across "different" sites, no named individual tester) — treat their specific numeric claims as low-confidence directional signal only, not verified fact. A genuine r/LocalLLaMA thread or ik_llama.cpp GitHub discussion specifically benchmarking tool-calling on a 16GB-card + 4-core-CPU rig was not found despite multiple targeted searches.

## What do people say about going from a large MoE back to a smaller dense model after experiencing latency/complexity issues with MoE CPU-offload setups — any documented regret/success stories either direction?

### Takeaway
One clear, specific, primary-source regret story was found (MoE → dense, for multi-step agent reliability, not primarily for CPU-offload latency) — but it was on a 32GB GPU with no CPU offload involved (both models fit on GPU). No story matching the exact scenario asked about (CPU-offloaded MoE abandoned specifically because of weak-CPU latency, reverting to dense) was found; the closest matches are about MoE's architectural reliability tradeoff, not CPU-offload mechanics specifically.

### Cited Findings
- The clearest documented regret story: switched to Ornith 1.0 35B (MoE) for its 3x single-shot speed advantage over dense Qwen3.6 27B, hit five distinct multi-step agent failures within two days (forgetting dependencies, contradictory assertions, mid-task abandonment, undefined function calls followed by false completion claims), and reverted to the dense model as default, reserving MoE only for single-shot tasks. — [wada-dev.com](https://wada-dev.com/en/blog/moe-vs-dense-local-llm-agent-tasks/) (RTX 5090 32GB, Ollama/opencode — no CPU offload involved, both models GPU-resident)
- Partial counter-example (success story for MoE, but GPU-resident not CPU-offloaded): on dual RTX 3090s, MoE Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B at 168.4 tok/s was judged clearly preferable to dense Qwen2.5-Coder-32B at 41.0 tok/s for "agentic coding workloads" and "local agent swarms" — but this setup had enough VRAM (2x24GB) to keep the MoE fully resident, sidestepping CPU-offload entirely. — [ure.us](https://ure.us/articles/best-local-llm-agentic-coding/)
- No GitHub issue, Reddit thread, or blog post was found describing someone specifically abandoning a `--cpu-moe`/`--n-cpu-moe` CPU-offloaded setup on a *weak CPU* in favor of a smaller fully-GPU-resident dense model, despite multiple targeted searches (including site:reddit.com queries, which returned no on-topic Reddit results at all — search access to r/LocalLLaMA discussion threads appears limited for this topic).

### Inferences
- The absence of a scenario-matched regret/success story, combined with the one real story found being driven by *reliability* (not latency) and occurring on hardware with no CPU-offload need, suggests the "MoE CPU-offload on weak CPU" pain point Mark is asking about may be under-discussed publicly relative to the generic "MoE vs dense for agents" discussion — possibly because most people hitting this specific combination (16GB GPU + 4-core CPU) either don't post about it, or self-select out of attempting large-MoE CPU-offload in the first place once they hit the throughput wall described in key question 1.
- Given the pattern in the one real story (format/state reliability degrading under the MoE's thinner per-token compute, independent of raw speed), Mark should weight the Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-specific malformed-tool-call reports (key question 4) as a stronger, more directly-applicable risk signal than generic throughput numbers when deciding whether to move to a dense model.

### Gaps
- Could not find a direct, scenario-matched (CPU-offload-latency-driven, not reliability-driven) regret story for the exact "weak CPU + CPU-offloaded MoE → dense" transition. This is the single biggest gap in this research — treat the lack of evidence as "not found," not as evidence that nobody has had this experience; r/LocalLLaMA-style community discussion of this specific pairing was not reachable through the search tools used.
